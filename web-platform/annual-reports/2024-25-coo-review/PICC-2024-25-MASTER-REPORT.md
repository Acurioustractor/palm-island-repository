## A message from our CEO

This year, for the first time in Queensland, decisions about Palm Island children sit with Palm Island people.

Bwgcolman Way went live. The Child Protection Act now lets our community make the calls — through our Bwgcolman blueprint, *Reclaiming our Storyline*. Decades of work behind it. Our team standing in front of it. And our children, finally, at the centre of it.

It is a moment I have been waiting for since 2007.

### Working with the community, not for the community

Eighteen years ago I started in this role with one belief: that PICC has to be of Palm Island, not just on it. That has not changed. What has changed is the size of what we can now do because of it.

This year we are 24 services. About 210 staff. Three out of four are Indigenous. Seven out of ten live on Palm. That is not a statistic to me — that is what community control actually looks like when you let it grow. Most of every PICC dollar pays a Palm Islander to deliver a service to another Palm Islander. That is how generational change happens. Not in a single budget cycle. In thousands of small, daily acts of work.

### What we built this year

A lot.

**The First 1,000 Days Program** opened its doors in April 2024 — a child health nurse, an Aboriginal health worker, and a GP, walking alongside families from before a baby is born to age two. Early childhood is fundamental for the help and the growth of the future of this Palm Island community. If we don't invest in our youth and in our children and in our babies, this community won't thrive.

**The Blue Card Liaison Service** ran its first full year as a pilot. Around twenty positive notices a month. Twenty community members, each month, walking out with the right to work with our kids, to take on kinship care, to volunteer. Doors that were shut, opening.

**Bwgcolman Healing Service** — the name our Elders chose — held its accreditation, ran 17,488 episodes of care, and kept doing what it does best: meeting people where they are.

**Our daycare team** rebuilt the Children and Family Centre after the floods took the equipment and damaged the building. Not "rebuilt" as in restored. Rebuilt as in came back stronger. Hailey and her team are the reason. The CFC is the front door of this organisation.

**Our NDIS service has tripled.** **The Digital Service Centre** finished its pilot with Telstra and twenty-one Palm Islanders on the floor — and we are now planning what comes next. **The Women's Healing Service** is taking First Nations women's voices behind the walls of TWCC and out into communities from the Torres Strait through to central Queensland. **Ferdy's Haven** has, for the first time in over thirty years, opened its men's groups alongside its women's groups.

And we are about to launch our own **PIC Leadership Program** — designed by our staff, for our staff. Building current leaders. Growing the next ones.

### What 20 years has taught me

In October 2027 PICC turns twenty. I keep thinking about what was asked at the daycare opening — *what are the grannies going to say about me?*

I hope what they say is this. *They worked with us, not for us. They invested in our babies. They built something we own. They didn't fly in and fly out — they stayed. And when the time was right, they handed it to us, with the lights still on.*

That is the work of the next twenty years too. Aged Care on Palm Island, so our Elders never have to leave Country. Bwgcolman Way expanded into health and justice. Every Palm Island story captured, consented, and held by us.

Three commitments. Three generations.

Our children are our future. They are our ancestors of tomorrow. Each of them have a role in growing our community and keeping our culture strong.

We've gotta make it right for them.

— **Rachel Atkinson**, CEO

## A message from our Chair

PICC belongs to the community.

That sentence is the whole of it, and the whole of why this year matters. In 2021 the community took ownership of this organisation in full. In 2024-25 the community took ownership of decisions about our own children. **Bwgcolman Way is now law on Palm Island** — the first time in Queensland that an Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Community Controlled Organisation has been granted Delegated Authority for child protection.

Distant governments telling Palm Island what to do and how to do it has never worked and will never work. This year is the proof.

### What the Board did this year

The Board's job is to keep PICC sharp, accountable, and ours. This year that meant:

- **Standing behind Bwgcolman Way as it went live** — and standing behind Rachel and her team as the work of it began.
- **Holding the line on local employment.** Three out of four PICC staff are Indigenous. Seven out of ten live on Palm. We do not move on these numbers. They are the point.
- **Backing two new launches** — the First 1,000 Days Program in April 2024, and the Blue Card Liaison Service pilot in July 2024. Both are about removing the barriers that have stopped Palm Island families from being able to look after Palm Island children.
- **Approving the PIC Leadership Program** that begins next month. Designed by our own people, for our own people. The next twenty years of PICC leadership starts here.
- **Listening to our Elders Group** — who sit at the heart of how this organisation makes decisions, not at the edge of it.

### What I see in the community

The positive changes we strive for are becoming evident — particularly among our young people, who are beginning to feel a sense of optimism about the future of Palm Island. I see it at the Daycare. I see it in the boys playing organised footy for the first time. I see it in the women coming back to Country with our Elders. I see it in the staff who, ten years ago, had never worked a day in a service like ours, and now run them.

### Where we go from here

In October 2027, PICC turns twenty. We will mark that milestone honestly. Twenty years of work. Twenty years of community control fought for and earned. And twenty more ahead.

I want to thank the Board, the Elders Group, Rachel and the staff, and every Palm Islander who has ever picked up the phone, walked through the door, or asked a hard question of this organisation. PICC is yours. It only works because you keep it that way.

— **Luella Bligh**, Chair

### Walking Country Together — since the year ended

In late 2025, after the close of the fiscal year this report covers, a group of Elders travelled from Palm Island back to the Hull River, near Mission Beach. We include the journey here because it is the natural epilogue to FY24-25 — the work the year was building toward.

For some, it was a return to family stories told but never seen. For others, it was the first time setting foot on land their ancestors had been forcibly removed from a hundred years before.

We are on our way up to Mission Beach to uncover a scenery that would happen back in 1918, said **Uncle Allan Palm Island.** Many old people said that our family were sent up there.

> *"Our mother was one of the stolen generation. You can just imagine the hardship that we had to go through."*
> — **Aunty Ethel Robertson**

The trip was Elder-led. Elder-paced. Elder-recorded. Photographers from PICC's on-Country studio walked with them. So did the Rangers from the Girringun Aboriginal Corporation. The conversations across the day moved from grief to recognition to laughter and back again.

This is the second year of the Hull River journey program. It is one of three forward commitments in our 20-year plan: a permanent on-Country journey rhythm so that every generation of Bwgcolman has a relationship with the places their families came from. Not a once-off. A practice.

> *"She didn't want us to be lonely like she did."*
> — **Aunty Cyndel Louise Pryor**

> *"Mum's never known her history."*
> — **Aunty Cyndel Louise Pryor**

The Elders Group is now planning the next journey for 2026, with help from Mizlam in PICC's research team and from the Rangers. Photos, stories, and an Elder-recorded language list from the 2025 trip are being archived inside the Empathy Ledger — a community-controlled digital archive — under restricted access until the Elders themselves decide how the work is shared.

This is what self-determination looks like in practice: Elders deciding the route, the pace, the recording, and the audience.

# PAGE 12 — ANCHOR STORY: BWGCOLMAN WAY

**Section label:** OUR ANCHOR STORY — Section bar in Ocean → Reef gradient.

### Decisions about our children, made by us.

In 2024-25, the Child Protection Act 1999 was used in a way it has never been used on Palm Island before. Under Part 2A of the Act — *prescribed delegates for Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander children* — the Queensland Government delegated statutory child-protection functions to the Chief Executive of an Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Community Controlled Organisation. **PICC became the first ATSICCO in Queensland to receive that authority.**

What does that mean in practice?

Decisions about whether a Palm Island child is removed, where they are placed, and how their family stays connected to them are no longer made by the state director of child safety in Brisbane. They are made on Palm Island. By Palm Islanders. Guided by our blueprint, *Reclaiming our Storyline* (April 2023), which the Queensland Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Child Protection Peak (QATSICPP) co-developed with the Department of Child Safety, Seniors and Disability Services.

> *"Bwgcolman means many tribes, one people. Our vision is that all Manbarra and Bwgcolman children are safe and cared for by family, nurtured by strong and enduring connections to their community, culture and Country."*
> — Bwgcolman Way blueprint

### What changed in FY24-25

- **10 staff** in the Bwgcolman Way team, integrated with the Family Care Service, Family Wellbeing Centre, Safe House, and Bwgcolman Healing.
- **Funding** — PICC's share of Queensland's $107.8 million four-year statewide Delegated Authority commitment.
- **First-year focus** — building decision-making capability with Elders, kinship carers, family group conferences, and front-line staff.
- **Recognition** — the Centre of Excellence at QATSICPP listed PICC as a Promising Practice case study.

### What it changed for one family

> *"Without Delegated Authority, a child this year would have been moved to mainland residential care. With Delegated Authority, the child stays with grandmother on Palm Island."*
> — De-identified case study, Bwgcolman Way team, FY24-25.

It is not a program. It is a transfer of decision-making authority. The legal responsibility still sits with the Queensland Chief Executive. The day-to-day decisions, and the cultural authority that goes with them, sit with us.

This is decades in the making. This is the work of the next twenty years.

# PAGE 13 — ANCHOR STORY: FIRST 1,000 DAYS

**[PHOTO: feature-first-1000-days/]** — choose strongest, ideally a child-health-nurse or AHW with a parent and baby. If unavailable, lay the page text-led with one supporting CFC photo.

### From conception to age two — wraparound, on Palm.

In April 2024, just before this fiscal year began, PICC opened the doors on the **First 1,000 Days Program**. A child health nurse, an Aboriginal health worker, and a GP, working alongside the Bwgcolman Healing Service, walking with families through the most formative period of a child's life.

The framing comes from Professor Kerry Arabena's 2014 *Medical Journal of Australia* paper, *The First 1000 Days: catalysing equity outcomes for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children* — the founding text of First 1000 Days Australia. We adopted the model and built the Palm Island version: a small, integrated team, deeply linked to early childhood (CFC) and to primary health (Bwgcolman Healing).

### What FY24-25 looked like

- **First full year** of operation.
- **Wraparound model** — antenatal through to age 2, including fathers, kinship support, and connection to early childhood services.
- **Linked into Bwgcolman Healing Service** for clinical care, immunisation, child health checks.
- **Linked into the CFC** for the warm-handover to early childhood education.

### Why it matters

Early childhood is fundamental for the help and the growth of the future of this Palm Island community, said **Rachel Atkinson**. *If we don't invest in our youth and in our children and in our babies, this community won't thrive.*

Every child has a right to feel safe. Every child has a right to access early childhood services. Every family has a right to have a sense of belonging and ownership of their future. The First 1,000 Days Program is the front of that promise.

# PAGE 14 — ANCHOR STORY: CFC daycare rebuild after the floods

**[PHOTO]** Hero image: `daycare-playground.jpg` or strongest from the rebuild reopening. Caption underneath: *Hailey Jane Wetzel and the daycare team, reopening week.*

### The barge brought the building. The floods nearly took it. The team brought it back.

The **Children and Family Centre** arrived on Palm Island by barge in June 2012. The CFC is the front door of PICC's family services — early childhood, primary health, family support, all under one roof. In late 2023 and early 2024, severe weather and flooding damaged the building and destroyed equipment.

The rebuild is the story of FY24-25.

> *"We flooded out, we lost all of our equipment. We came together as a team — but as a community as well."*
> — Childcare staff

> *"It was really hard, because we flooded out, we lost all of our equipment."*
> — **Hailey Jane Wetzel**, CFC Manager

### What happened

- Daycare relocated temporarily during repair.
- Playgroup was closed for weeks while floors and furniture were replaced.
- Equipment was sourced through Logistics, with help from staff personal networks across Palm and Townsville.
- The Centre reopened for the new fiscal year — fully equipped, fully staffed.

### What it taught us

- Local employment is what made the rebuild possible. Outside contractors would not have been on hand the day the water dropped.
- The CFC is more than a building. The team is the building.
- Community resilience is not a metaphor. It is paid work, done by named people, on the days it is hardest.

### Eight risks we are watching

Naming a risk is not admitting failure. It is the precondition for navigating one. Publishing them publicly is an act of accountability to community, members, and partners.

1. **Funding fragmentation** — too many small contracts, each with its own compliance load.
2. **Compliance burden** — disproportionate for a remote ATSICCO; staff capacity finite.
3. **Trust and reputation** — a single negative public moment can echo for years on a small island.

5. **Scope and governance depth** — 24 services demand more board capacity than any single director can carry alone.
6. **Colonial trauma and lateral violence** — the system PICC operates inside still produces both.
7. **Coordination load for the 20-year celebration** — the most under-recognised risk to FY26-27.
8. **Data sovereignty and outcome measurement** — outcomes data is held by funders. PICC needs its own measurement infrastructure.
