## Chapter 2: Manbarra Country (Pre-1914)

### Narrative

For thousands of years, the Manbarra people lived on these islands — part of the broader Wulgurukaba nation, the "canoe people" of the Halifax Bay coast. Their creation stories speak of Gabbul, the carpet snake, who shaped the land and waterways.

Before European contact, the Manbarra population was around 200 people living in deep connection with land and sea. The bêche-de-mer (sea cucumber) trade from the 1850s brought outsiders, displacement, and disease. By the early 1900s, the population had fallen to roughly 50.

In 1912, the island was briefly a holiday resort. Two years later, it would become something very different.

## Chapter 3: The Reserve (1914)

### Narrative

J.W. Bleakley, Queensland's Chief Protector of Aboriginals, selected Palm Island as "a penitentiary for troublesome cases." Under the *Aboriginals Protection and Restriction of the Sale of Opium Act 1897*, the government could remove any Aboriginal person to any reserve, for any reason, without appeal.

What had been a holiday resort just two years earlier became an instrument of control. Over the next six decades, thousands of Aboriginal people from across Queensland — speaking more than 40 different languages — would be forcibly relocated to Palm Island.

Many were sent here as punishment: for speaking their language, for being "troublesome," for loving the wrong person, or simply for being Aboriginal in a place where the government wanted them gone.

### Key Facts

- **Date gazetted**: 20 June 1914
- **Architect**: J.W. Bleakley, Chief Protector of Aboriginals
- **Legal basis**: *Aboriginals Protection and Restriction of the Sale of Opium Act 1897*
- **Purpose (Bleakley's words)**: "a penitentiary for troublesome cases"

## Chapter 4: Hull River (1914-1918)

### Narrative

While Palm Island was being established, another settlement already existed 200km south — Hull River, on the traditional lands of the Djiru people near Tully. By 1917, 490 Aboriginal people were confined there under Superintendent Kenny.

Conditions were catastrophic. Malaria swept through the camp, killing approximately 200 people in 1917 alone. There was no adequate medical care, no escape.

**Then, on 10 March 1918 at 10 PM, a Category 5 cyclone struck. Winds of 240–288 km/h. A 12-foot storm surge. The settlement was obliterated.**

Superintendent Kenny and his daughter were killed. Up to 100 Aboriginal people died — the true number was never properly counted. The survivors, traumatised and with nothing left, were transferred to Palm Island in June 1918.

They carried with them their languages, their knowledge, their grief, and their determination to survive.

### Key Facts

- **Location**: Hull River, Djiru country, near Tully, North Queensland
- **Peak population**: 490 (1917)
- **Malaria deaths**: ~200 in 1917
- **Cyclone date**: 10 March 1918, 10 PM
- **Cyclone category**: 5 (Innisfail cyclone)
- **Wind speed**: 240–288 km/h
- **Storm surge**: 12 feet (3.7m)
- **Deaths in cyclone**: Superintendent Kenny + daughter, up to 100 Aboriginal people
- **Transfer to Palm Island**: June 1918

## Chapter 5: Many Tribes, One People (1918-1972)

### Narrative

Over more than fifty years, the Queensland government forcibly relocated Aboriginal people from every corner of the state to Palm Island. More than 3,950 individual removals are documented in government records — the true number is certainly higher.

People arrived speaking different languages, carrying different songlines, from rainforest and reef, desert and river country. They were expected to simply become one community overnight, under the absolute authority of a government superintendent.

Instead, they did something remarkable. Elder Dick Palm Island coined the word **"Bwgcolman"** — meaning *many tribes, one people*. It became the name for this new, forged-in-adversity community. Not a replacement for their original identities, but an acknowledgment of what they had built together.

## Chapter 6: Life Under Government Control

### Narrative

Palm Island was run by a succession of government-appointed superintendents who held absolute power over every aspect of residents' lives. They controlled who could marry, who could work, who could leave, and who would be punished.

**Robert Curry (1918–1930)** was superintendent for twelve years. Under his rule, residents were subjected to floggings and exile to Eclipse Island as punishment. His tenure ended in tragedy in 1930 when he dynamited his own home and shot the settlement doctor before taking his own life.

Later superintendents continued the pattern. Under Bartlam, residents described living in "Gestapo times." The **dormitory system (1923–1975)** forcibly separated children from their families — part of the Stolen Generations that scarred Aboriginal communities across Australia.

Every night at 9 PM, the electricity was cut. A bell rang. Curfew was enforced. For decades, this was life on Palm Island — total institutional control over a people who had never surrendered their sovereignty.

### Key Superintendents

| Name | Period | Notable |
|------|--------|---------|
| Robert Curry | 1918–1930 | Floggings, Eclipse Island exile, 1930 tragedy |
| Bartlam | 1930s–1940s | "Gestapo times" |
| (others) | 1940s–1970s | Research needed |

### Key Facts

- **Dormitory system**: 1923–1975 (children separated from families)
- **9 PM curfew**: Electricity cut, bell enforced
- **Eclipse Island**: Used as punishment exile location
- **Curry's death**: 1930 — dynamited home, shot doctor, took own life

## Chapter 7: The Strike of 1957

### Narrative

On 10 June 1957, Palm Island residents launched a five-day strike — one of the earliest and most significant acts of organised Aboriginal resistance in Australian history.

The **"Magnificent Seven"** who led the strike were: Willie Thaiday, Albie Geia, Eric Lymburner, Sonny Sibley, Bill Congoo, George Watson, and Gordon Tapau.

The government's response was swift and brutal. At 4 AM, police conducted a dawn raid. The seven leaders were handcuffed and tied to the mast of a boat. They and their families were banished from the island — scattered across the state as punishment for daring to resist.

The strike failed to change conditions immediately. But it planted a seed. It proved that resistance was possible, that solidarity could hold even against overwhelming force. It became a founding story of the modern Aboriginal rights movement.

### Key Facts

- **Date**: 10 June 1957
- **Duration**: 5 days
- **The "Magnificent Seven"**: Willie Thaiday, Albie Geia, Eric Lymburner, Sonny Sibley, Bill Congoo, George Watson, Gordon Tapau
- **Government response**: 4 AM dawn raid, handcuffed, tied to boat mast
- **Punishment**: Leaders and families banished from Palm Island

## Chapter 8: Fantome Island (1928-1973)

### Narrative

Just off Palm Island lies Fantome Island, home to two institutions that cast long shadows over the community.

The **Lock Hospital (1928–1945)** confined Aboriginal people diagnosed with sexually transmitted infections. Patients were transferred under police escort, often with no understanding of why they were being taken. The **Lazaret (1939–1973)** housed people with Hansen's disease (leprosy), run by religious orders.

When the Health Department finally closed the Lazaret in 1973, they burned the buildings. But the graves remain. Most Palm Island families have at least one family member buried on Fantome Island.

Today, Fantome Island is a place of deep cultural significance — a site of remembrance, grief, and the enduring bonds between the living and the dead.

### Key Facts

- **Lock Hospital**: 1928–1945 (STI confinement)
- **Lazaret**: 1939–1973 (Hansen's disease/leprosy)
- **Run by**: Religious orders (Sisters of Our Lady Help of Christians)
- **Closed**: 1973 — buildings burned by Health Department
- **Legacy**: Graves remain; most Palm Island families have connections

## Chapter 9: Mulrunji (2004)

### Narrative

On 19 November 2004, Cameron Doomadgee — known to his community as Mulrunji — was arrested on Palm Island. He was dead within 60 minutes of entering the police station. He was 36 years old.

The autopsy revealed injuries consistent with severe force — a ruptured liver and portal vein, four broken ribs, and a black eye. Yet the arresting officer, Senior Sergeant Chris Hurley, was acquitted of all charges in 2007.

In the immediate aftermath, 400 community members took to the streets. The courthouse and police station were burned. Mulrunji's death was the 147th Aboriginal death in custody since the 1991 Royal Commission.

In 2016, the police response to the community uprising was ruled racially discriminatory by the court. In 2018, Queensland reached a $30 million settlement with the community.

Mulrunji's death — and the community's refusal to accept it in silence — became a turning point. It galvanised calls for justice, accountability, and the urgent need for communities to control their own affairs.

### Key Facts

- **Date**: 19 November 2004
- **Victim**: Cameron Doomadgee (Mulrunji), age 36
- **Time in custody**: Dead within 60 minutes of arrest
- **Injuries**: Ruptured liver and portal vein, 4 broken ribs, black eye
- **Officer**: Senior Sergeant Chris Hurley — acquitted 2007
- **Community response**: 400+ protested, courthouse and police station burned
- **Significance**: 147th Aboriginal death in custody since 1991 Royal Commission
- **Legal outcomes**: Police response ruled racially discriminatory (2016), $30M settlement (2018)
