## Executive Summary

Palm Island, located 65 kilometers off the coast of Townsville in Queensland, Australia, represents one of the nation's most compelling narratives of Indigenous resistance, survival, and contemporary resurgence. Home to approximately 2,138 people (89.7% Indigenous), the island's journey from colonial "punishment reserve" to community-controlled self-determination offers critical lessons in health equity, cultural preservation, and Indigenous-led development.

### The Health Challenge

Palm Island faces profound health disparities rooted in historical trauma and ongoing social determinants of health:

| Indicator                | Palm Island         | Comparison                           |
|--------------------------|---------------------|--------------------------------------|
| Life expectancy gap      | ~11 years lower     | vs. non-Indigenous Australians       |
| Type 2 diabetes          | 20–30% of adults    | vs. 5.3% nationally                  |
| Housing shortfall        | 100+ households     | with overcrowding 15–20 per dwelling |
| Median weekly income     | $306                | vs. $660 Queensland average          |

### The Community Response

The **Palm Island Community Company (PICC)**, established in 2007 and achieving full community control in September 2021, has emerged as a model for Indigenous-led health and social service delivery. Under CEO Rachel Atkinson's leadership, PICC has grown from a single employee to approximately 197 staff (95% local Palm Islanders), generating $5.8 million in wages and $9.75 million in economic output annually.

**PICC delivers integrated services including:**

- Primary health care through **Bwgcolman Healing Service**
- Early childhood services (0-8 years)
- Family wellbeing and domestic violence response
- Youth services and mental health support
- Social enterprises creating employment

This document traces Palm Island's history through a health and wellbeing lens, documenting the challenges, initiatives, and outcomes that characterize this community's journey toward self-determination.

## Historical Context

### Traditional Ownership: The Manbarra People

The **Manbarra people** (also known as Wulgurukaba) are the traditional owners of Great Palm Island and the surrounding island group. According to Manbarra Dreamtime stories, the Palm Island group was formed from fragments of the Rainbow Serpent, establishing a sacred connection to Country that predates European arrival by tens of thousands of years.

When Captain Cook sailed past in 1770, approximately 200 Manbarra people lived on the islands, maintaining their traditional way of life, speaking two dialects (Buluguyban and Mulgu) of the Nyawaygic language family. Their traditional territory included the Palm Island group and mainland areas around present-day Townsville.

By the 1890s, most Manbarra had been forcibly removed to the mainland through colonial policies, setting the stage for Palm Island's transformation into a place of exile for Aboriginal peoples from across Queensland.

### Colonial Era: The "Punishment Reserve" (1918–1984)

#### The Hull River Tragedy (1918)

On **March 10, 1918**, a devastating cyclone destroyed the Hull River Mission near Mission Beach, killing approximately 100 people. In **April 1918**, survivors were forcibly relocated to Palm Island, marking the beginning of the island's role as Queensland's primary Aboriginal settlement.

Under Queensland's **1897 Aboriginal Protection Act**, Palm Island was officially designated as "a penitentiary for troublesome cases" by Chief Protector J.W. Bleakley. Over the following decades, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples from across Queensland were forcibly removed to the island, often as punishment for "infractions" against restrictive colonial policies.

#### The Multi-Tribal Community

By the 1930s, Palm Island's population had grown from 200 to over 1,630 people representing at least **57 different language groups**. This extraordinary convergence of displaced peoples created an unprecedented situation—and from this trauma emerged a new collective identity: **Bwgcolman** (meaning "many tribes, one people"), a term coined by Manbarra Elder Dick Palm Island to unite these disparate communities.

#### Living Conditions and Health Impacts

The mission era (1918–1970s) was characterized by:

**Physical Conditions:**

- Tin sheds and substandard housing
- Lack of running water in many homes
- Outdoor toilets and minimal electricity
- Overcrowding (10+ people in small dwellings)
- Inadequate food rations instead of wages

**Health Consequences:**

- High rates of infectious disease due to overcrowding
- Malnutrition from inadequate rations
- Dental disease from poor diet
- Mental health impacts from trauma and control
- Intergenerational health disadvantage

**Governance Control:**

- Morning roll calls and nightly curfews
- Bell tower signaling daily routines
- Superintendents with extensive powers
- Strict limits on movement and freedom
- Forced labor without wages

#### The Magnificent Seven and Resistance (1957)

The most significant act of resistance came on **June 10, 1957**, when seven men led a five-day strike demanding fair wages instead of rations:

1. **Albert "Albie" Geia**
2. **Willie Thaiday**
3. **Eric Lymburner**
4. **Sonny Sibley**
5. **Bill Congoo**
6. **George Watson**
7. **Gordon Tapau**

Superintendent Roy Bartlam had threatened to deport Geia for allegedly disobeying a European overseer. The strike spread community-wide, with residents refusing to work under exploitative conditions.

After five days, armed police from Townsville conducted dawn raids. The "Magnificent Seven" were arrested at gunpoint with their families, shackled, and forcibly deported to different Aboriginal settlements across Queensland—never to return together.

> **Their courage sparked a movement that eventually led to wage justice for Indigenous workers across Australia.** June 9 is now commemorated annually on Palm Island.

#### Health Legacy of the Mission Era

The mission era left lasting health impacts that continue to affect the community:

- **Intergenerational trauma** from family separations, forced labor, and abuse
- **Educational disadvantage** limiting health literacy
- **Economic poverty** restricting access to healthy food and housing
- **Cultural disruption** severing connections to traditional health practices
- **Distrust of government services** affecting health-seeking behavior

### Path to Self-Governance (1984–2007)

Significant changes began in the 1970s:

| Year             | Milestone                                                                                 |
|------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| 1975             | Children's dormitories closed (operating since 1922)                                      |
| 1984             | Last vestiges of Protection Act abolished                                                 |
| 1985             | Queensland Government relinquished control; DOGIT transferred land to community           |
| March 30, 1985   | Palm Island Aboriginal Council established with five elected councillors                  |
| January 1, 2005  | Council became Palm Island Aboriginal Shire Council with full local government powers     |

Despite these advances, health and social challenges persisted. The community needed organizations capable of delivering culturally appropriate services designed and controlled by Palm Islanders themselves.

> **Source:** [Queensland Government - First Nations Cultural Heritage](https://www.qld.gov.au/firstnations/cultural-awareness-heritage-arts/community-histories)

## PICC Formation and Evolution

### Establishment (2007)

The **Palm Island Community Company (PICC)** was established in **2007** through a dual shareholder model:

- **Queensland Government:** 50% ownership
- **Palm Island Aboriginal Shire Council:** 50% ownership

**Rachel Atkinson**, a proud Yorta Yorta woman with deep connections to her Aboriginal heritage, was appointed as the first CEO—and the organization's only employee.

Rachel's leadership philosophy stemmed from her family lineage of tireless activists, including her great uncle **William Cooper** and **Sir Douglas Nicholls** (Australia's first Indigenous Governor of South Australia). Her professional background included nearly a decade (1996-2006) leading the Townsville Aboriginal and Islander Health Service (TAIHS).

**PICC's Mission:**

1. Deliver human and social services
2. Build community capacity
3. Stimulate economic development

### Growth Phase (2007–2021)

From that single-employee beginning, PICC embarked on steady expansion:

**Service Integration:**

- Assumed responsibility for multiple government-funded programs
- Developed integrated health, family, and early childhood services
- Created "no wrong door" approach—any entry point connects to all services

**Local Employment Strategy:**

- Prioritized hiring Palm Islanders for all positions
- Invested in training and professional development
- Built career pathways within the organization
- Achieved **95% local workforce**

**Economic Impact by 2021:**

| Metric                 | Value         |
|------------------------|---------------|
| Employees              | ~180          |
| Local wages annually   | $5.8 million  |
| Economic output        | $9.75 million |

**Health Services Expansion (August 2021):**

PICC took a major step by assuming responsibility for Palm Island primary health services, amalgamating their existing health center with the Townsville Hospital and Health Service primary health center to create an integrated community-controlled Aboriginal Medical Service—**Bwgcolman Healing Service**.

### Community Control Achievement (September 2021)

On **September 30, 2021**, after years of lobbying by CEO Rachel Atkinson and the PICC Board, all services, workforce, and assets were transferred to a new company that maintained the Palm Island Community Company name but was now **fully owned by Palm Islanders**.

Palm Island Mayor and past PICC chairperson **Mislam Sam** described this as:

> "A hard-won achievement for the Palm Island community... The community, its elders, and leaders have worked for decades for self-determination."

**Significance for Health and Wellbeing:**

- Community now controls its own health service design and delivery
- Services can be tailored to cultural needs and priorities
- Local employment in health services creates economic benefit
- Health workforce reflects community it serves
- Self-determination itself improves health outcomes through empowerment

### Current Leadership Structure

**CEO:** Rachel Atkinson (since 2007)

- Co-Chair, Queensland First Children and Families Board
- Co-Chair, Family Matters Queensland
- Board Director, SNAICC
- Deputy Chairperson, Queensland Aboriginal and Islander Health Council

**Chairperson:** Luella Bligh

- Led transition to community control
- Emphasizes strong professional governance

**Traditional Owner Director:** Allan Palm Island

- Manbarra representative
- Cultural knowledge holder
- Master of Fine Arts (RMIT University Melbourne, 1999)

**Board Members** include community members with professional expertise in Indigenous governance, legal matters, finance, and education.

## Health and Wellbeing Outcomes

### Life Expectancy Gap

**National Comparison (2020–2022):**

| Population                                      | Life Expectancy |
|-------------------------------------------------|-----------------|
| Australian average                              | 83.2 years      |
| Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander average   | 71.9 years      |
| **Gap**                                         | **~11 years**   |

Palm Island's life expectancy is likely at or below the Indigenous average due to:

- Remote location limiting access to specialist services
- Concentrated socioeconomic disadvantage
- Higher chronic disease burden
- Historical trauma impacts

### Chronic Disease Burden

#### Diabetes

| Metric                      | Palm Island Estimate | National Average |
|-----------------------------|----------------------|------------------|
| Type 2 Diabetes Prevalence  | 20–30% of adults     | 5.3%             |
| Age of Onset                | 30s–40s              | 50s+             |

**Complications prevalent in Palm Island:**

- Cardiovascular disease
- Kidney disease (requiring dialysis)
- Diabetic retinopathy (blindness)
- Peripheral neuropathy
- Foot ulcers and amputation
- Premature death

**Management Challenges:**

- Limited endocrinology access on island
- High cost of healthy food
- Medication compliance barriers
- Complications requiring mainland treatment
- Multi-morbidity (multiple conditions)

#### Cardiovascular Disease

- Leading cause of death for Indigenous Australians
- High rates of rheumatic heart disease (legacy of overcrowding and untreated infections)
- Hypertension common
- Earlier onset than non-Indigenous population
- Risk factors: smoking, diabetes, obesity, stress

#### Kidney Disease

- High prevalence linked to diabetes and hypertension
- Progression to end-stage renal disease requiring dialysis
- Limited dialysis facilities on Palm Island
- Patients often must relocate to Townsville
- Significant family and cultural disconnection

#### Respiratory Disease

High rates of:

- Asthma (children and adults)
- Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)
- Bronchiectasis
- Acute respiratory infections

Contributing factors include overcrowded housing, high smoking rates, and limited specialist access.

### Mental Health

#### High Burden

- Depression and anxiety very common
- Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) from historical and ongoing trauma
- Substance use disorders
- Suicide risk (especially young men)
- Complex trauma from intergenerational experiences

#### Contributing Factors

- **Intergenerational trauma:** Stolen Generations, mission era, forced family separations
- **Ongoing racism and discrimination**
- **Poverty and socioeconomic disadvantage**
- **Overcrowding and lack of privacy**
- **Unemployment and lack of opportunity**
- **Family violence exposure**
- **Grief and loss** from premature deaths
- **Incarceration impacts**

#### Protective Factors

- Cultural connection and identity
- Family and community support
- Employment and purpose
- Hope and opportunity
- Access to timely, culturally appropriate support
- Community-led prevention programs

## Healthy Eating Initiatives

### Challenges to Healthy Eating on Palm Island

| Challenge                     | Description                                                                    |
|-------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **High food costs**           | Remote location requires barge transport; prices significantly higher than mainland |
| **Limited availability**      | Fresh fruit, vegetables, and dairy have short shelf life; often unavailable    |
| **Poverty**                   | Low incomes ($306/week median) limit purchasing healthy options                |
| **Overcrowded kitchens**      | Limited cooking facilities shared by 15–20 people; meal preparation difficult  |
| **Processed food prevalence** | Longer shelf life, lower cost, and availability make processed foods default   |
| **Lost cultural foods**       | Bush tucker knowledge declining; access to traditional foods limited           |
| **Health literacy**           | Gaps in nutrition education and understanding                                  |

### Outcomes and Impact

While comprehensive outcome data is limited, observed improvements include:

- Increased breastfeeding initiation and duration
- Better-nourished children in early childhood programs

- Cultural connection through traditional foods
- Crisis response capacity (demonstrated in 2024 floods)

- **Structural cost issues:** Freight costs remain fundamental barrier
- **Poverty:** Income levels limit food choices regardless of knowledge
- **Housing:** Overcrowding limits meal preparation capacity
- **Supply chain:** Reliance on barge transport vulnerable to weather/disruption
- **Sustainability:** Programs depend on ongoing funding

## Key Innovations and Outcomes

### Integrated Service Delivery Model

PICC's most significant innovation is the integration of health, family, and social services under one community-controlled organization:

**"No Wrong Door" Approach:**

- Entry through any service connects to all services
- Case coordination across programs
- Holistic family support
- Reduced duplication and gaps

**Local Workforce (95% Palm Islanders):**

- Services delivered by community members
- Cultural safety built in
- Employment creates economic benefit
- Knowledge stays in community
- Trust and relationships

> **Key Outcome:** Significant reduction in the number of Aboriginal children being removed from families on Palm Island—demonstrating that community-controlled, integrated services can address child safety while keeping families together.

### Storm Recovery Innovation (February 2024)

The February 2024 floods devastated Palm Island, destroying homes and displacing families. PICC's response became a documented innovation model:

**Seven Innovation Programs:**

1. **Movember Men's Recovery Program** — $1.9M secured for five years
2. **Experimental Collapsible Beds** — Innovative emergency housing solutions
3. **Community-led Washing Machine Distribution** — Practical support coordination
4. **Orange Sky Mobile Laundry Partnership** — External partnership integration
5. **Quality Food Distribution Network (Woolworths)** — Food security during crisis
6. **Elder-Led Recovery Governance** — Traditional decision-making in emergency
7. **Systematic Story Documentation** — 26 stories captured for learning

- Local government authority
- Mayor: **Alf Lacey**

- Councillors include: Telstan Sibley, Germaine Bulsey, Ebanese Oui

- Cultural authority maintained alongside contemporary governance

## Closing the Gap Progress

### Relevant Targets

The National Agreement on Closing the Gap includes targets directly relevant to Palm Island:

| Target               | Goal                    | Palm Island Status          |
|----------------------|-------------------------|-----------------------------|
| Life Expectancy      | Close gap               | ~11 year gap persists       |
| Child Mortality      | Reduce deaths           | Higher than average         |
| Health Outcomes      | Achieve parity          | Significant disparities     |
| Year 12 Completion   | Increase to 96%         | 15% completion rate         |
| Employment           | Increase rate           | 29.1% unemployment          |
| Housing              | Eliminate overcrowding  | 100+ household shortfall    |

### Community-Led Progress

While statistical gaps remain significant, Palm Island demonstrates community-led approaches that address root causes:

**Self-Determination:**

- PICC's transition to full community control (2021)
- Community designing and delivering own services
- Cultural authority in decision-making
- Local employment strategy

**Economic Development:**

- Digital Service Centre creating jobs
- Social enterprises building wealth
- $5.8M in local wages annually
- $9.75M economic output

**Health System Transformation:**

- Community-controlled primary health (Bwgcolman Healing Service)
- Integration of health with social services
- Cultural healing approaches
- Aboriginal Health Workers from community

**Early Investment:**

- Comprehensive 0-8 years services
- School readiness programs
- Developmental screening and early intervention
- Reduced child protection involvement

**Cultural Strength:**

- Language revival programs
- Elder-led knowledge transmission
- Connection to Country programs
- Cultural identity development

### The Challenge Ahead

Closing the Gap on Palm Island requires sustained investment in:

1. **Housing:** Eliminating the 100+ household shortfall
2. **Economic opportunity:** More employment beyond current services
3. **Education:** Pathways to Year 12 completion and beyond
4. **Health services:** Specialist access, chronic disease management
5. **Infrastructure:** Renewable energy, improved transport, digital access
6. **Cultural programs:** Continued support for language and cultural revival
